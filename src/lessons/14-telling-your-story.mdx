The technical rounds get you to 'maybe'. The behavioral round gets you to 'yes'. At senior level they're checking one thing: <mark>would I trust this person with an ambiguous, important problem?</mark> You answer that with stories, and you've got good ones.

## What senior behavioral rounds measure

- **Scope and impact**: did the work matter beyond your ticket?
- **Ambiguity**: what did you do when nobody knew the answer?
- **Influence**: changing minds without authority.
- **Ownership**: incidents, mistakes, follow-through.
- **Growing others**: mentoring, raising the bar.
- **Judgment**: trade-offs, saying no, knowing when 'good enough' is right.

## STAR, tightened

**S**ituation: 2 sentences of context. **T**ask: your specific responsibility. **A**ction: <mark>about 70% of the answer, in 'I' language</mark>: what you decided, why, and what you said to whom. **R**esult: numbers, outcomes, and what you learned or would change.

The most common failure is 2 minutes of situation and 20 seconds of action. Aim for 2–3 minutes total.

<Order>

Put a strong STAR answer in order.

1. Context in two sentences: team, system, stakes
2. Your specific responsibility and what made it hard
3. What you did and why, including the trade-offs you weighed
4. Outcome with numbers
5. What you learned / would do differently

<Why>

Ending on the lesson learned shows self-awareness, and it's often what the interviewer writes down.

</Why>

</Order>

## Build a story bank

Prepare 6–8 stories you can bend toward many questions. Go through these prompts and write one line from your own career for each:

| Story prompt | Answers questions about |
| --- | --- |
| Something you helped build or scale from an early stage | Scaling, ambiguity, building from scratch |
| A time you led people, formally or not | Leadership, mentoring, delegation, conflict |
| A legacy system you rewrote, migrated, or untangled | Technical judgment, risk, migrations, influence |
| A design or abstraction you're proud of (and its trade-offs) | Architecture, judgment |
| An integration or dependency that broke in production | Reliability, incidents, ownership |
| A feature where correctness really mattered (money, data, security) | Rigor, cross-team work |
| Something you started without being asked (tooling, process, AI workflows) | Initiative, leverage, staying current |

<Note>

For each one, write down a number (time saved, customers onboarded, incidents reduced, PR cycle time) and one thing that went wrong. Stories with friction are more believable.

</Note>

## The questions you will get

- **Tell me about yourself**: 90 seconds, present → past → why this role. Not a CV recital.
- A conflict with a colleague or manager.
- A failure or a production incident you caused.
- A decision made with incomplete information.
- Pushing back on a product or technical direction.
- The project you're proudest of.
- **Why are you looking?** Keep it forward-looking and positive.

<Quiz>

Your company just had layoffs and the mood is uncertain. Best answer to 'Why are you looking?'

- [ ] Complain about leadership
- [ ] Say you're not really looking
- [x] 'There's been some reorganization, and it made me think about what I want next: [specific thing this role offers]. That's why this role caught my attention.'
- [ ] Talk mostly about salary

<Why>

Honest, brief, no bitterness, and it pivots to what you're moving toward. Interviewers want to hear what pulls you to them, not what pushes you away.

</Why>

</Quiz>

<Myth fact={false}>

In behavioral answers, say 'we' throughout to show you're a team player.

<Why>

Credit the team for context and results, but the Action has to be 'I'. The interviewer is hiring you and needs to know what you specifically did and decided.

</Why>

</Myth>

## Questions to ask them

Good questions show judgment and help you decide:

- 'What would make someone in this role clearly successful after six months?'
- 'What's the hardest technical problem the team is facing right now?'
- 'How are technical decisions made and written down?'
- 'How does on-call work, and what was the last major incident like?'
- 'What changed on the team in the past year?' (a gentle way to ask about stability)

<Interview>

Tell me about a time you had to make an important technical decision with incomplete information.

<Rubric>

- Clear, brief situation with real stakes
- Specific about their own role and the uncertainty involved
- Explains how they reduced uncertainty (spikes, data, asking experts) and the options weighed
- Made the call with reversibility in mind (two-way vs one-way door), communicated it
- Concrete result, ideally quantified
- Reflection: what they learned or would change; uses 'I' for actions

</Rubric>

<Answer>

**S:** 'At a fast-growing hotel platform, we had to decide whether to keep extending our booking engine or rebuild the pricing piece, with a big partner launch six weeks out.'

**T:** 'As tech lead, I owned the recommendation.'

**A:** 'We didn't know how pricing load would grow. I ran a one-week spike with production traces to estimate it, listed options with their reversibility, and chose to isolate pricing behind an interface so we could swap it later. That was a two-way door. I wrote it up and got buy-in from product by showing the launch risk of each option.'

**R:** 'We launched on time; three months later we swapped the implementation in two weeks without touching callers.' **Learned:** 'Separate the reversible decisions from the irreversible ones early.' (Use your real numbers.)

</Answer>

</Interview>

<CheatSheet>

- Senior = trusted with ambiguous, important problems.
- STAR with ~70% Action, 'I' for actions, numbers in Results.
- 6–8 stories, each with a number and some friction.
- Why looking: forward-looking, brief, positive.
- Ask questions that help you decide too.

</CheatSheet>
